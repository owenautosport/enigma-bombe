Bletchley Park · 1940–1945 · how Enigma was broken

Inside the Bombe

Alan Turing's Bombe didn't read German messages. It did one narrower job very fast: it threw out every Enigma setting that couldn't possibly be right, until only a handful were left for people to check by hand. This page explains how it worked, step by step.

1

The Enigma

Pressing a key on an Enigma sent an electric current on a long path through the machine. At the end of the path a different letter lit up on the lampboard. The path had three kinds of part:

Rotors

Three wired wheels chosen from a box of five. The right one steps on every key press, like a car's mileage counter, so the wiring is different for every letter.

Reflector

A fixed wheel at the end that sends the current back through the rotors by a different route.

Plugboard

Ten cables at the front, each swapping a pair of letters on the way in and again on the way out. This is where most of the security came from.

How many settings?

Two weaknesses

It works both ways. If E encrypts to T at some position, then T encrypts to E at that same position. That made Enigma convenient to use, because the same settings encrypted and decrypted. The Bombe relies on it too.

A letter never encrypts to itself. The reflector can't send a current back down the wire it came in on, so E can never come out as E. It sounds minor, but it gave the codebreakers a free test.

2

The crib

The Bombe needed a starting point: a crib, which is a guess at a piece of the plaintext. German operators were very predictable. Weather reports said WETTERVORHERSAGE ("weather forecast"). Daily reports said KEINEBESONDERENEREIGNISSE ("nothing special to report").

But where in the message does the crib go? Slide it along the ciphertext. Because a letter can never encrypt to itself, any position where a crib letter sits on the same cipher letter is impossible. Try it below. The ciphertext was made with a working Enigma simulation.

3

The menu

With the crib lined up, each column gives a link between two letters. For example, "at step 5, E and T are linked". Draw each letter as a dot and each link as a line labelled with its step, and you get a graph. Bletchley called this the menu, because it told the Bombe operators how to wire up the machine.

Loops are what give the Bombe its power. A branch that leads nowhere can't contradict anything. A loop has to come back to where it started, so it can be checked. The more loops a menu had, the fewer false alarms the machine gave.

4

Turing's trick

The plugboard seems to make everything hopeless, since every letter passes through an unknown swap on the way in and on the way out. Turing split the machine into the part you don't know (the plugboard) and the part you can test (the rotors and reflector, which he called the scrambler):

Enigma at step n = Plugboard → Scramblern → Plugboard

so if the crib says P becomes C:   plug(C) = Scramblern( plug(P) )

You can't know every plug, but you can guess one and follow what it implies around a loop. Here is the loop E → T → R → E from the menu (steps 5, 4, 9), at the true rotor setting. These are real letters from the simulation:

Guess: E is plugged to P
plug(E)P scrambler
step 5 plug(T)H scrambler
step 4 plug(R)M scrambler
step 9 plug(E)P back to P ✓ consistent
Guess: E is plugged to A
plug(E)A scrambler
step 5 plug(T)V scrambler
step 4 plug(R)Z scrambler
step 9 plug(E)O O ≠ A ✗ contradiction

The first guess is right: in the real key E is plugged to P, T to H and R to M. The loop checked all of this without knowing anything else about the plugboard. That removes the 150-trillion factor. What's left is 60 rotor orders × 17,576 positions, which a machine can work through.

5

The machine

The Bombe does that loop check in electricity, for every guess at once:

Scramblers

Each stack of three drums is an Enigma without a plugboard. There is one stack for each link in the menu, and each is set a fixed number of steps ahead to match that link's step number.

26-wire cables

The scramblers are cabled together following the menu. Each cable has 26 wires, one for each letter, so it carries all 26 possible plug partners of a letter at once.

The test

A voltage goes onto one wire of the test letter's cable, for example "E is plugged to A". The current spreads through every consequence of that guess, and a register shows which of E's 26 wires lit up.

How many wires light up tells the machine what to do. These three results come from running the menu above through a simulated Bombe:

This is the clever part. A wrong guess spreads to almost every wire, because contradictions multiply. If exactly one wire stays dark, that wire must be the true plug partner, even though the guess itself was wrong. So one voltage tests all 26 possible partners at once.

If the result is anything other than 1 or 25 lit wires, the setting is impossible. The drums step on and the test repeats automatically. The machine went through all 17,576 positions for one rotor order in roughly 20 minutes, and stopped whenever it found a setting that was consistent.

6

Welchman's diagonal board

Gordon Welchman noticed something the first design didn't use: plugboard cables are symmetric. If E is plugged to P, then P is plugged to E. He added a board that connected wire P in E's cable to wire E in P's cable, for all 325 pairs of letters. Drawn as a grid, those connections mirror across the diagonal, which is where the name comes from.

Every connection gives the current a new route, so a wrong guess hits a contradiction much sooner. The board mattered most for short menus with few loops, which were common. Here is the same crib cut to different lengths, searched across all 17,576 positions of the correct rotor order:

Stops in the simulation, drawn on a log scale. With 12 links, the board cut 735 stops to 4. With the full 16 links (5 loops) the menu is strong enough that even without the board there are only 2 stops, and with it the only stop is the true setting.

7

After the stop

A stop is not the full key. It is a candidate: a rotor order, drum positions and a few plug pairs. For this example the Bombe stopped at drum positions P G F, with these plugboard deductions coming straight from the wires:

E–PT–HR–MS–XW–LZ–K G unpluggedV unplugged

That is 6 of the 10 real plug pairs, all correct, before anyone has touched a real Enigma.

Record the stopWrite down the drum positions and the plug pairs the machine found.
Check itSet up a checking machine (an Enigma copy) and decrypt part of the message.
Fill the gapsWhere German words appear, work out the remaining plug pairs and the ring settings by hand.
Read the dayWith the day's key found, every message on that network for the rest of the day could be read.

One catch. The Bombe assumed only the fast rotor turned during the crib. If the middle rotor stepped partway through, the menu was wrong. So cribs were kept to around 12–16 letters, and failed runs were repeated assuming a step at a different point.

Timeline

  1. 1932

    Marian Rejewski of the Polish Cipher Bureau works out Enigma's rotor wiring using mathematics and some captured documents.

  2. 1938

    The Poles build the bomba. It exploits a German habit of typing each message key twice at the start of a message.

  3. 1939

    Five weeks before the war, the Poles hand everything to Britain and France. Turing designs a new machine that works from cribs instead, so it doesn't depend on German key habits.

  4. 1940

    The first British Bombe, "Victory", arrives at Bletchley Park in March. It was built by the British Tabulating Machine Company, with Harold "Doc" Keen as engineer. In May the Germans stop typing keys twice, and the Polish method stops working. In August a Bombe with Welchman's diagonal board goes into service.

  5. 1943

    US Navy Bombes, built in Dayton, Ohio, take on the four-rotor naval Enigma used by U-boats.

  6. 1945

    About 200 British Bombes are running. Most were dismantled after the war. A working rebuild now runs at Bletchley Park.
